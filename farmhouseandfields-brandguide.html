1   What Makes Farmhouse & Fields Special

The Farmhouse already has a very clear feel to it[cite: 10]. The website does not need to invent one[cite: 10]. It needs to show what Melissa has built: a small, consistent group of dogs, a place that feels comfortable and familiar, lots of room to play, and someone who knows and loves the dogs like her own[cite: 11].

At the Centre

Low, consistent numbers[cite: 14, 16]. Familiar dogs[cite: 14, 16]. Lots of room[cite: 14].

Farmhouse & Fields keeps the group small and familiar[cite: 16]. Melissa gets to know each dog incredibly well: who they play with, how they settle in, what they love, and when they need a little space[cite: 17].

Melissa Knows These Dogs

That comes through all over her Facebook page[cite: 22]. She knows their friendships, routines, quirks and personalities, and she clearly loves having them there[cite: 22].

That connection is one of the strongest parts of the business and it should be visible[cite: 23].

The Farmhouse Itself

The house, yard, barn and fields are part of the experience[cite: 26]. It is the "home away from home" for these pups and is a big part of what makes the experience unique[cite: 27].

Warm and Easy

People should feel welcome here, but they should also be able to find what they need without digging: how daycare works, what the day looks like, pricing, the wait-list, policies, and what happens next[cite: 29].

In Practice: Red & Ink

  • Red for key moments: Buttons, links, small accents, and the occasional detail we want people to notice first[cite: 54].
  • Ink for reading: Navigation, body copy, forms, pricing, instructions, and anything that needs to be immediately clear[cite: 61].

In Practice: Blue, Oat & Photos

  • Blue and oat in supporting roles: Useful for quieter backgrounds, small sections, cards, or details[cite: 60]. Neither needs to compete with the red[cite: 60].

How We Use Fraunces & DM Sans

  • Fraunces for headings: Page titles, section headings, and short lines where a little personality helps[cite: 80]. Warm and distinctive without turning the site into a "country" theme[cite: 70].

4   Photography

Her photos are one of the best parts of the brand[cite: 93]. Melissa is fabulous at capturing the dogs as they actually are[cite: 94]. Her Facebook page already tells the story of the Farmhouse through play, friendships, seasons, funny moments, the property, and the dogs' individual personalities[cite: 95]. We should use that[cite: 96]. So rich and fun and brings in an amazing storytelling element[cite: 96].

1. Use real photos throughout

Not just in a gallery at the end, but work them into the home page, About page, daycare information, and anywhere they help show what a day actually looks like[cite: 101].

2. Let the photos tell stories

Use the muddy ones, the goofy ones, the beautiful ones, group shots, quiet moments, and dogs with their favourite friends[cite: 104]. They do not all need to look polished[cite: 105].

3. Keep editing light (but primarily zero)

Crop for layout and make small corrections when needed, but everything else stays the same[cite: 107].

4. No stock photos, ever

Tons of amazing photos makes the job so much easier[cite: 110]. Use the photos throughout the site, build a standalone gallery, and pull in a box from Facebook for the day-to-day stories[cite: 97].

5   Voice

The website should sound like Melissa[cite: 112]. Her Facebook page already has the right voice[cite: 113]. It is warm, direct, and full of little stories because she actually knows these dogs—their friends, their habits, their quirks, what they love, and what makes them themselves[cite: 113]. She clearly loves what she does, and that should come through without dressing it up[cite: 114].

What We Carry Into The Website

Specific dogs, real moments and down to earth language[cite: 117]. A little humour when the moment calls for it[cite: 117]. There is so much material to weave a beautiful little story[cite: 119].

Melissa knows and loves these dogs like her own[cite: 115, 125]. We want that to come through in the stories and details, not in a pile of marketing words[cite: 115].

What We Avoid

No lofty pet-care language, no trying to sound luxe, and no endless dog puns[cite: 118].

Skip words like "premium," "curated," and "exceptional" when plain language says it better[cite: 131].

1. Tell real stories

Her Facebook works because she tells us what actually happened: who found a friend, who lives for the pool, who came in ready to wrestle, or who had a very strong opinion about a costume[cite: 122].

2. Let the connection show

Melissa knows and loves these dogs like her own[cite: 125]. We do not need to announce that on every page; the stories and specifics should make it obvious[cite: 125, 126].

3. Keep the language normal

Warm and confident, but never lofty[cite: 131].

4. Be clear when it matters

Pricing, hours, policies, wait-list information, and next steps can simply be written plainly[cite: 132].

7   Keeping It Consistent

A few things worth keeping consistent[cite: 149]. Nothing here needs to be complicated[cite: 150]. These are just the choices that give us a solid starting point for the website and anything Melissa makes later[cite: 150].

1. Keep the site light: White first, with red as the strongest accent[cite: 155]. Let the photos carry a lot of the colour[cite: 155].

2. Use the real place: Farmhouse, yard, barn, fields, seasons, and dogs[cite: 156]. We don't need extra "farmhouse" decoration[cite: 156].

3. Keep type simple: Fraunces for headings, DM Sans for everything people need to read quickly[cite: 159]. Let the logo keep its own script[cite: 159].

4. Use real photos only: Build from Melissa's gallery[cite: 165].

5. Keep the words warm and real: Specific stories, normal language, and no lofty pet-care copy[cite: 162].

6. Make the practical stuff easy: Pricing, hours, services, wait-list, policies, and next steps should always be easy to find[cite: 168].
